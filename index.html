    01Grid forecast auditOct – Nov 2026

    US grid forecasts looked like they were getting worse. Three errors in the public data explain it.

    A 10-year audit of day-ahead demand forecasts for 50+ US grid operators in EIA-930, the federal government’s hourly grid data: 6.1M hourly records, an analysis plan committed to Git before any result, and 138 automated tests.

    What gets forecast

    Every hour of 2025, laid flat.

    Every day, each grid operator forecasts tomorrow’s demand hour by hour and reports it to EIA-930. This is what they were forecasting: demand peaks on summer afternoons, with the year’s high on 29 July, and bottoms out early on a Sunday morning in May.

    Figure 2: a heat map of 2025, one column per day and one row per hour, colored by demand. Summer afternoons are brightest.

    2025, as published

    19 of 53 operators lost to a forecast that just repeats yesterday.

    Each bubble is one operator, sized by its 2025 demand and placed by forecast skill: how much its day-ahead forecast beats a naive one that repeats demand from 24 hours earlier. Left of zero, the naive forecast wins. California ISO and Southwest Power Pool, two of the six largest, both lose.

    The plan called for checking the largest operators that lose to the naive forecast against the operators’ own published data. Both that could be checked turned up problems.

    Errors 1 and 2California ISO

    California’s demand ran an hour late, and counted battery charging.

    EIA-930’s forecast for the California ISO equals the operator’s own, but its demand matches the operator’s actual load an hour earlier: on 333 of 334 days checked in 2025, and for about four of the ten years in all.

    It also counts battery charging that the forecast leaves out. On an average 2025 day the gap is widest at midday, when the batteries charge, and through 2025 it follows charging almost megawatt for megawatt (R² = 0.91).

    Show the numbers

    Corrected · California ISO

    Move demand back an hour, take the charging out, and the forecast fits.

    The hour shift is checked against the operator’s own load data, and the charging comes from the battery figures California ISO reports in the same EIA-930 files. Its 2025 error drops from 8.28% to 2.35%.

    Error 3Southwest Power Pool

    Southwest Power Pool’s forecast changed on 7 May 2025.

    Before 7 May, the forecast EIA-930 carries for SWPP was within 2% of SPP’s own day-ahead forecast on 122 of 125 days. After it, the two were more than 2% apart on 219 of 238 days, while SWPP’s demand kept matching. Why it changed isn’t visible in the files.

    The median day’s error jumps from 1.5% before 7 May to 10.8% after. With SPP’s own forecast swapped in, the median day after 7 May is 1.4%.

    Show the numbers

    2025, corrected

    Fix the three errors, and 19 becomes 17.

    California ISO’s skill goes from −0.78 to 0.56 and Southwest Power Pool’s from −0.79 to 0.54. No other operator changes.

    Corrected · national

    And the national rise goes flat.

    As published, national error rises 0.061 percentage points a year (Mann–Kendall p = 0.049), and 2025 is the worst of the ten years. Corrected, it trends 0.009 points a year (p = 0.721), and 2025 falls to 8th-worst.

    Where it stops: even as published, the median operator showed no clear trend, and three of five pre-registered robustness checks didn’t confirm the rise. The corrections came after the pre-registered results, and only operators that looked bad were checked against their own data, which tilts the corrected trend down. The write-up says so.

    02Grounding probesAug 2026

    Grounding DINO + SAM finds “the dog.” It mostly misses “not.”

    Text-prompted segmentation handles “the dog.” I wanted to know whether it handles “the dog that is not on the sofa,” “the cup left of the plate” or “the red one.” I built a benchmark of 914 human-verified probes across 608 images from COCO, LVIS and Visual Genome, covering negation, spatial relations, attribute binding and fine-grained categories.

    03Drift-aware MLOpsFeb 2026

    Models fail quietly when the data shifts. This pipeline notices, retrains, and checks before it ships.

    I built a pipeline that serves an XGBoost model behind FastAPI, logs every prediction to PostgreSQL, watches for covariate and concept drift with Evidently, Prometheus and Grafana, and retrains through Airflow and MLflow.

    The retraining loop

    Serve, log, watch, retrain, gate, ship.

    1. Serve XGBoost behind FastAPI
    2. Log every prediction to PostgreSQL
    3. Watch for drift with Evidently, Prometheus and Grafana
    4. Retrain in Airflow, tracked in MLflow
    5. Gate at least +0.005 PR-AUC on shifted data, no more than 0.03 ROC-AUC lost on historical data
    6. Ship the new model takes over

    Simulated concept shift

    It caught the drift and recovered PR-AUC from 0.37 to 0.89.

    In a simulated concept shift, the pipeline caught the drift, retrained and recovered PR-AUC on the shifted data from 0.37 to 0.89, with historical ROC-AUC holding at 0.75.

    Figure 10: bars. PR-AUC on shifted data, 0.37 before retraining and 0.89 after. ROC-AUC on historical data, 0.75 before and after.

    16 slices in, one image out.

    Our 3D-to-2D compressor condenses the 16 CT slices into one image for an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18.

    The schematic stack folds flat into a single image, which feeds ResNet-18 to decide real or tampered.
